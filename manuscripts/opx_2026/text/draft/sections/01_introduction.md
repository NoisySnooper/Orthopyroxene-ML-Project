# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The thermobarometry problem

Quantitative estimates of the pressure and temperature at which magmas crystallized are foundational to igneous petrology. Crystal-liquid and mineral-mineral thermobarometers translate mineral and melt compositions into P-T conditions, and thirty years of calibration work has produced a robust classical toolkit for crystals grown at equilibrium with their host liquid (Putirka, 2008; Neave and Putirka, 2017; Wieser et al., 2022). These calibrations are regressions of experimental data on a small number of physicochemical predictors chosen for thermodynamic interpretability, typically the jadeite solubility equilibrium for pressure and the Fe-Mg exchange equilibrium for temperature. The classical calibrations are transparent, reproducible, and defensible because every term in the regression has a petrologic meaning.

They also have known limits. Putirka (2008) reports root-mean-square errors of roughly 45 °C and 2.7 kbar on the calibration set for cpx-liq, and comparable figures for opx-liq, with the caveat that these are self-validated errors computed on the same data used to fit the regression (Jorgenson et al., 2022, identify this as a systematic underestimate of generalization error in classical thermobarometers). Classical barometers also fail hardest where the calibrations are weakest: high-silica melts (Masotta et al., 2013), ultramafic-to-intermediate compositions (Neave and Putirka, 2017), and pressures above ~15 kbar where experimental coverage thins. Mineral-only thermobarometers that do not require a coexisting liquid composition are particularly difficult to calibrate classically because the P-T information has to be inferred from the mineral composition alone, without the thermodynamic anchor of a melt in equilibrium.

## 1.2 Machine learning thermobarometry: opportunity and open problems

A sequence of ML thermobarometer papers has exploited the growth of open experimental databases (ExPetDB, LEPR) and the availability of tree-based and gradient-boosted regressors to produce data-driven thermobarometers that compete with or exceed classical calibrations on held-out test sets. Petrelli et al. (2020) established the random-forest approach for cpx thermobarometry; Jorgenson et al. (2022) optimized the extra-trees variant and distributed a public package; Higgins et al. (2022) extended the approach with uncertainty quantification; and Ágreda-López et al. (2024) added analytical error propagation, a post-hoc bias correction for the "regression to the mean" phenomenon of tree ensembles, and a web-app for applied users. The Ágreda-López et al. (2024) cpx pipeline reports test-set RMSE of 57 °C and 2.5 kbar for cpx and 36 °C and 2.1 kbar for cpx-liq, competitive with classical calibrations on their held-out test.

Two gaps in the existing literature motivate this work.

**First, the orthopyroxene case is underserved.** All four of the papers cited above target clinopyroxene as the primary mineral. Opx-liq classical thermobarometers exist (Putirka, 2008, equations 28a and 29a) but no opx-specific ML thermobarometer has been published. This is a gap: orthopyroxene is common in arc magmas, mantle peridotites, and continental intraplate basalts, and the high-temperature opx-opx-liq intersection is a central constraint on mantle potential temperature (Walter, 1998; Herzberg, 2004). The case for an opx ML thermobarometer is not that opx is more tractable than cpx but that it requires its own evaluation on its own experimental corpus.

**Second, evaluation protocols in existing ML thermobarometer papers are inconsistent.** Jorgenson et al. (2022) use GroupKFold with citation as the group. Ágreda-López et al. (2024) use StratifiedGroupKFold with citation-grouped 10-fold cross-validation, which is methodologically preferable and is the protocol we adopt here. However, neither work pre-registers evaluation bins or acceptance criteria for bias correction, and neither reports regime-stratified performance against the honesty bar that non-overlapping 95% CIs would impose. The field's default reporting convention is aggregate test-set RMSE, which can mask strong regime-dependent performance heterogeneity, particularly for opx, where deeper-mantle samples (> 30 kbar) are scarce in experimental databases and large-magnitude errors in those regimes can be averaged out of an aggregate headline.

## 1.3 This work

We present an ML thermobarometer for orthopyroxene, trained on ExPetDB (Hirschmann et al., 2008; curation updated 2025-07-21) with citation-grouped test splits, evaluated under a pre-registered regime-stratified protocol locked on 2026-04-17 before any post-hoc correction fitting. The pipeline covers four (track, target) cells: opx-liq T and P, and opx-only T and P. Four contributions distinguish this work from the prior ML thermobarometer literature.

**(i) First opx-specific ML thermobarometer.** The best tuned model per cell is, in order of pipeline: ElasticNet on raw oxide fractions for opx-liq T (RMSE 77.06 °C [CI 61.81, 92.73]); MLP on raw features for opx-liq P (4.40 kbar [CI 3.08, 4.54]); LightGBM on additive log-ratios for opx-only T (146.63 °C [CI 121.19, 170.00]); and Random Forest on pairwise log-ratios for opx-only P (10.35 kbar [CI 6.90, 13.92]). The opx-only P case is the pipeline's strongest single result after bias correction; opx-liq T is reported as a null result against filtered Putirka 28a in §4.4.

**(ii) Nine-family model sweep including TabPFN v2.** We evaluate nine model families (RF, ERT, XGB, Histogram GB, CatBoost, LightGBM, ElasticNet, MLP, and TabPFN v2 as a pretrained foundation-model peer) under an identical 20-seed protocol. Four different tuned families win the four (track, target) opx cells pre-correction, indicating no single architecture dominates at these sample sizes (n = 600 to 1035). TabPFN v2 (Hollmann et al., 2025) is included with default hyperparameters and raw oxide features only; details of its protocol differences appear in §3.4.

**(iii) Pre-registered regime-stratified evaluation with dual robustness check.** The four-bin pressure partition (0-5, 5-15, 15-30, ≥30 kbar) with labels shallow_crustal, deep_crustal_MASH, lithospheric_mantle, deeper_mantle was locked on 2026-04-17 with petrological rationale tied to Hildreth and Moorbath (1988), Annen, Blundy, and Sparks (2006), and Klemme and O'Neill (2000), before any bias correction was fit. A directional claim of the form "method A beats method B in regime X" requires (1) non-overlapping 95% bootstrap confidence intervals on pooled RMSE and (2) the 97.5th-percentile per-seed RMSE for method A to fall below method B's lower CI, at a regime with at least 20 samples. This dual robustness check is pre-registered and reported in the main text alongside all numeric claims.

**(iv) Pre-registered tolerance-band acceptance rule for post-hoc bias correction.** We test two correction forms on out-of-fold training residuals: a regime-piecewise linear correction (Form A, our contribution motivated by the pre-registered pressure regimes and the Zhang and Lu, 2012, bias-corrected random forest framework) and the Ágreda-López et al. (2024) quantile-thresholded piecewise-sigmoid correction (Form B). Acceptance is decided under a pre-registered tolerance-band rule. A correction for a (track, target) cell is accepted if aggregate test-set RMSE improves beyond numerical tolerance and no pre-registered regime with n ≥ 20 degrades by more than the larger of an absolute floor (10 °C for T, 1 kbar for P) or 10 % of the pre-correction regime RMSE. Regimes with n < 20 are logged but exempt from the veto. The tolerance-band values sit below the reported standard errors of estimate for classical opx thermobarometers (Putirka, 2008, Table 4.1: 26-38 °C for temperature calibrations, 2.5-3.2 kbar for pressure calibrations), so a per-regime degradation within the tolerance band is indistinguishable from the measurement noise of the target itself. Under this rule, three of the four opx cells accept a Form A correction (opx-liq P, opx-only T, opx-only P); opx-liq T accepts Form B marginally at canonical seed only and fails the 20-seed majority vote, which we report as a null. The headline opx-only P result is a 41.9 % aggregate RMSE reduction (10.35 → 6.05 kbar), robust across all 20 seeds.

**(v) Honest reporting of null results.** We report three honest nulls alongside the headline shipped claims. Form B (Ágreda-López et al., 2024 style) accepts on zero of four opx cells across the 20-seed majority vote and on one of four cells at canonical seed; it is not rescued by the 15× Gaussian composition-noise augmentation protocol that enables it in the Ágreda-López et al. (2024) cpx pipeline. Opx-liq temperature is effectively a null: classical Putirka 28a (filtered to 161 plausible rows) remains competitive with ML at 71.67 °C vs 77.06 °C. The deeper-mantle regime for opx-liq has only n = 8 samples, below the n ≥ 20 honesty bar and reported as insufficient data for any directional claim.

## 1.4 Paper structure

Section 2 describes the experimental data (ExPetDB), preprocessing, citation-grouped train/test split, and the pre-registered four-bin pressure partition with its dual robustness check. Section 3 describes the nine model families, Optuna hyperparameter search, 20-seed protocol, feature-engineering schemes, bias-correction Form A and Form B fit on out-of-fold residuals, the tolerance-band acceptance rule, and the augmentation sensitivity ablation. Section 4 reports aggregate and regime-stratified performance, the bias-correction scorecard, head-to-head against Putirka (2008) classical opx thermobarometers, feature attribution via SHAP, and augmentation-ablation results. Section 5 discusses the regime interpretation, the temperature-vs-pressure asymmetry, the Form A vs Form B outcome and its geologic interpretation, and limitations. Section 6 concludes. The evaluation framework (pre-registration, per-seed acceptance decisions, SHA256 data hashes) is archived with the submission for reviewer verification.
